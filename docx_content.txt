VELO-X UX Framework &amp; Interaction Design Progressive Discovery for the Solo Cycling Adventurer Version 2.0 • March 2026 Includes Fuerteventura Deep-Dive Case Study 1. Product Vision 1.1 Core Purpose Velo-X is a route discovery and planning platform designed for solo cyclists exploring new regions. It serves two primary jobs: Job 1: Help the cyclist find, define, and commit to their next cycling experience. Job 2: Be the cyclist’s planning companion through the adventure. Velo-X does not accompany the rider on-bike (no real-time GPS). It is a planning-focused platform that builds confidence and commitment before the ride. 1.2 Primary User The solo cyclist exploring new regions. Their biggest challenge is not a lack of routes—it is decision paralysis and uncertainty. They are alone, heading somewhere unfamiliar, and need enough confidence to commit. Every UX decision must serve this emotional arc: Curiosity → Confidence → Commitment 1.3 The Psychological Adventure This is the central design philosophy. The user’s journey through Velo-X should mirror the emotional arc of an actual adventure: the thrill of discovery, the narrowing of possibilities, the building of anticipation, and finally the decisive commitment. Routes are not the starting point. Routes are the payoff. Showing routes too early is like reading the last chapter of a novel first—it kills the journey. The user must first fall in love with a place, understand its character, feel its geography, and only then discover the specific paths they can ride. The platform sells the dream first, the logistics second, and the routes last. 1.4 Design Principles Progressive revelation. Information is layered through a strict hierarchy: Globe → Region → Sub-geography → Routes. Each layer deepens the user’s emotional investment before revealing more detail. Seasonal intelligence. The platform is always aware of time and surfaces contextually relevant content. Base Camp model. The booking unit is a base (hotel + transport), not a route. Routes are bookmarked options explored from that base. Alive, never dormant. Even in off-season, the platform shows cycling activity somewhere and cultivates desire. Confidence over features. Every element must reduce the solo cyclist’s uncertainty about an unfamiliar destination. 2. The Navigation Hierarchy 2.1 The Core Principle: Routes Are Secondary Most cycling platforms lead with routes. Velo-X inverts this. Routes are secondary and tertiary content that the user reaches only after passing through a very limited vector of selection. The hierarchy is strict and intentional: Level View What the User Sees Psychological Purpose L1 Globe The planet. Heatmap glow. Seasonal pulse. Wonder. “The world is riding.” L2 Region Canary Islands, Andalucía, Transylvania. Character, season, vibe. Attraction. “This place calls to me.” L3 Sub-geography Fuerteventura, Sibiu, Málaga. Zones, bases, landscape identity. Familiarity. “I can picture myself here.” L4 Routes &amp; Hotspots Specific rides, elevation profiles, GPX, logistics. Confidence. “I know what I’ll ride.” The user cannot skip levels. Each level earns the right to show the next. This is not a limitation—it is the adventure itself. 2.2 Level 1: The Globe The globe is the primary entry point and discovery engine. It combines Strava heatmap data with seasonal intelligence to show the user where the world is cycling. No routes are visible at this level—only regions glowing with activity. Default State: The Globe Already Knows The globe never asks. It infers today’s date the moment the user opens Velo-X and immediately shows the world as it is right now. No onboarding screen, no “select your dates” prompt, no empty state. The globe is alive from the first pixel. Regions in peak cycling season for today glow warmly. Off-season regions appear muted (cooler tones, reduced opacity). The algorithm considers: temperature, daylight hours, heatmap recency (current month, not annual averages), altitude accessibility, and local events. The user sees the planet’s cycling heartbeat in real time without lifting a finger. Calendar Override: “But What About June?” The calendar is an override, not a starting point. A subtle, non-intrusive date control (date picker or natural language input: “second half of June”, “any weekend in April”) lets the user ask “what if?” without breaking the flow. The moment a date is entered, the entire globe repaints: heatmap intensity shifts to reflect historical activity for that period, regions off-season for the selected window fade, and regions in their sweet spot glow. The user can clear the override at any time to snap back to today’s reality. Seasonality Toggle: See the Raw World The seasonality layer is a lens, not a lock. A simple toggle (untick) lets the user remove the seasonal intelligence and see the raw heatmap—pure cycling activity data, unfiltered by weather, daylight, or accessibility. Every region shows its full-year activity without the platform making judgments about what’s “good right now.” This serves multiple user types. The explorer who is flexible and wants to discover destinations they’d never consider under seasonal curation. The hardcore cyclist who rides in any conditions and doesn’t want the platform filtering out “off-season” places. The curious user who wants to compare raw popularity before layering in seasonal reality. In all cases, the user can re-enable the seasonality layer at any time and discover seasonality post-factum—seeing how conditions overlay onto the places that already caught their eye. The Three Globe States The globe operates in three combinable states: Default — Auto-inferred today’s date + seasonality layer active. What most users see on first open. Zero input required. Calendar override — User’s hypothetical dates + seasonality layer active. “Show me the world as it will be when I’m free.” Raw heatmap — Seasonality unticked. Pure activity data, unfiltered. Every region shows full-year cycling popularity. The user discovers seasonality post-factum by re-enabling the layer. States 1 and 2 can each be combined with or without the seasonality layer, giving the user full control. The critical design constraint: the toggle must be subtle and non-disruptive. It should not clutter the globe interface. A small icon or checkbox near the calendar control is sufficient—power users will find it, casual users won’t be confused by it. 2.3 Level 2: Region When the user clicks a glowing area on the globe, the view begins to zoom in. But it does not jump to routes. It lands on a Region view—a geographic and editorial overview of a cycling macro-region. Examples of regions: Canary Islands, Andalucía, Transylvania, Sardinia, Croatian Coast, Norwegian Fjords. The Region view communicates: Character. What makes this region special for cycling? Volcanic landscapes, Mediterranean coastlines, mountain passes, wine country? Seasonal window. “Canary Islands: year-round. Best: February–May and October–November.” Sub-geographies. Clickable areas within the region, each with a distinct identity. For the Canaries: Tenerife, Gran Canaria, Fuerteventura, Lanzarote. Vibe signal. Is this a pro-training destination? A gravel adventure? A scenic leisure ride? A mix? Social proof. “3,200 cyclists rode here last March.” No routes are shown. No elevation profiles. No GPX. The user is still in the dream. 2.4 Level 3: Sub-Geography The user selects a specific place within the region. This could be an island, a city, a valley, a mountain range, or a coastal section. The globe’s zoom transition continues smoothly—the globe becomes a map, the map becomes a detailed geographic view. The Sub-Geography view is the pivotal screen. Here, the user transitions from dreaming to planning. This view communicates: Zones. The sub-geography is divided into distinct cycling zones, each with its own character, difficulty profile, and landscape identity. These are geographic, not route-based. Base options. Suggested towns or areas to stay, with accommodation signals and transport access. Conditions. Weather, wind patterns, road quality, water availability, bike shop proximity. Hotspots. Iconic landmarks, must-see viewpoints, famous climbs—teased but not fully revealed as routes yet. At this level, the user is building a mental map of the place. They understand where the flat coastal rides are, where the mountain challenges live, where the gravel adventures hide. They can see the character of each zone and start imagining themselves there. 2.5 Level 4: Routes, Hotspots &amp; Specific Information Only after the user has selected a zone or a base within a sub-geography do routes appear. Routes are the payoff—the reward for the user’s investment in understanding the place. Route information at this level includes: detailed elevation profile, distance and estimated duration, surface type per segment, difficulty grade with comparisons, GPX download, logistics confidence signals (water, phone signal, bike shops), recent activity and ratings, and weather overlay for the user’s selected dates. Routes are bookmarked into the Base Camp collection—a flexible menu of adventures, not a rigid itinerary. The user builds a wish list from this level. 2.6 The Globe-to-Map Transition The transition from globe to map is continuous, not a page change. When the user selects a region (L2), the globe begins to zoom in. When they select a sub-geography (L3), the zoom continues. When they enter a zone (L4), the map is fully detailed. At every point, the user can zoom back out to regain context. The spatial continuity maintains the sense of exploration—the user is always oriented on the planet. Both the calendar context and the seasonality toggle state are inherited at every level. If the user set “June” on the globe, every subsequent view shows June-relevant data. If the user unticked the seasonality layer, subsequent views show unfiltered data—though seasonal information remains available on demand as a reference overlay the user can enable at any deeper level. 3. Quick Ideas — For the Uninspired 3.1 Purpose Not every user arrives with a destination or date. Quick Ideas are bundled suggestion cards that sit below or beside the globe, providing ready-made sparks for cyclists who think “I could do something in the next month or two but I don’t know what.” Each card is a complete decision cluster—everything the cyclist needs to go from “hmm” to “yes” in one glance. Importantly, clicking a Quick Idea card takes the user to Level 2 (Region) or Level 3 (Sub-geography), not directly to routes. The progressive revelation is preserved. 3.2 Card Structure Where: Region or sub-geography name and key visual. When: Specific optimal window (e.g., “March 18 – April 12”), not a vague season. Conditions: Temperature range, daylight hours, expected weather. Social proof: How many cyclists rode there in this period last year. Logistics: Transport options and cost from user’s region, accommodation range. Countdown: “Window opens in X days.” Natural urgency. 3.3 Card Behavior Monthly rotation. Cards show options 1–3 months ahead. Geographically aware. A user in Romania sees reachable, affordable options first. Calendar-responsive. If the user enters a date on the globe, Quick Ideas filter to match. Peak-surfacing. The most compelling cards highlight time-limited windows about to open. 3.4 Interconnection: Globe + Calendar + Quick Ideas User Type Entry Point System Response Has dates, no destination Calendar: “June 10–20” Globe repaints. Quick Ideas filter to best June options. Clicking takes user to L2/L3. Has destination, flexible dates Zooms into Sardinia Calendar highlights optimal months. Quick Ideas show nearby alternatives. Totally uninspired Opens the app Globe has already inferred today. No input needed. Quick Ideas show best upcoming 1–3 month windows. Off-season dreamer Opens in December Globe shows southern hemisphere alive. Quick Ideas show “Spring is 68 days away.” 4. The Base Camp Model 4.1 The Insight The cyclist’s real decision is not “which route do I ride?” It is “where do I plant myself, and what can I ride from there?” The hotel is the anchor. Routes are options explored from that anchor. Bookmarking a route is not committing to it. The only hard commitment is booking the base: hotel + transport to get there. 4.2 The Base View When the user selects a base (town or area) within a sub-geography, the Base View shows: Accommodation options. Hotels, B&amp;Bs, cycling-friendly stays with ratings and prices. Transport. How to get there from the user’s city. Flights, ferries, trains, bike logistics. Route radius. A map showing all bookmarkable routes radiating from the base, organized by effort: Easy morning rides (&lt; 40 km) Full day adventures (60–100 km) Epic challenges (100+ km) Weather overlay. Forecast for the user’s selected dates, mapped against route difficulty. Bike infrastructure. Rental shops, repair services, bike transport within the area. 4.3 Flexible Wish List Bookmarked routes form a collection—a menu of adventures, not a schedule. On the trip, the cyclist opens their bookmarks each day, checks weather and mood, and picks a ride. This freedom is what the solo adventurer wants. 5. Solo Cyclist Confidence Signals A solo cyclist in unknown territory has specific fears. Every route page (L4) must implicitly answer: Fear Question Velo-X Answer Getting lost “Will I find my way?” GPX download, waypoint descriptions, offline map. Breakdown “What if something breaks?” Nearest bike shop, emergency contacts, phone signal. Basic needs “Where do I eat/drink?” Water and food markers on every route. Overcommitting “Is this too hard for me?” Difficulty rating with comparisons. Wrong timing “Right time to go?” Seasonal suitability score per route. Isolation “Anyone done this recently?” Recent heatmap activity, ratings. 5.1 Logistics Confidence Signals Water availability frequency (e.g., “every 20 km”) Phone signal quality (reliable / intermittent / none) Nearest bike shop with distance from route midpoint Road surface type per segment (asphalt, gravel, sand, mixed) Daylight margin (“At your pace, finish 2h before sunset”) Wind advisory for exposed segments 6. Seasonal Engagement &amp; Desire Activation 6.1 Core Principle The world is cycling somewhere right now, and your next adventure is taking shape whether you act or not. 6.2 Winter Paradigm When the user’s region is dormant, the globe shows life elsewhere. Southern hemisphere glows. Countdowns surface: “Mallorca enters season in 47 days.” Historical data creates anticipation: “Last March, 2,400 cyclists rode in Andalucía.” 6.3 Scarcity of Experience Build desire around real truths, not manufactured urgency: Weather windows. “Transfăgărășan is rideable June 15 – Oct 1. Last year, only 58 of 108 days had ideal conditions.” Daylight. “June in Norway: 16h of light, enough for a 200 km Arctic ride. By August, you’ve lost 4 hours.” Accommodation. “Sibiu: 34 cyclist-friendly stays. In July, 80% booked 3 weeks ahead.” 6.4 Living Bookmarks Bookmarked routes are not static. Surface micro-updates: new cafés on saved routes, road improvements, recent ratings. The user’s bookmarks are alive and waiting. 6.5 Year-Round Loop Season User State Velo-X Activates Key Emotion Winter Dreaming Globe shows global activity. Countdowns. Wish list. Longing Early Spring Planning Regions entering season. Weather windows. Base suggestions. Excitement Peak Season Riding Daily picks from bookmarks. Weather overlay. Logging. Freedom Autumn Reflecting Trip stats. “12 of 18 routes ridden.” Seed next year. Satisfaction 7. Deep-Dive Case Study: Fuerteventura This case study walks through the complete Velo-X experience for a solo cyclist discovering Fuerteventura, demonstrating every level of the navigation hierarchy and the psychological journey at each stage. 7.1 The User Marta, 34, from Bucharest. Intermediate road cyclist, some gravel experience. Looking for a warm escape in February. Has one week of annual leave. Opens Velo-X on a dark January evening with no specific destination in mind. 7.2 Level 1: The Globe What Marta sees The globe has already inferred today’s date: January. No prompt, no question. Northern Europe is dark. The Mediterranean coast has a faint glow. But the Canary Islands are pulsing warmly—year-round cycling season, strong heatmap activity. Marta sees this instantly. Then she overrides: she enters “Feb 15–22” in the calendar. The globe repaints: the Canaries glow even brighter. A Quick Idea card appears: “Canary Islands • February. 19–24°C. 11h daylight. 1,800 cyclists last February. Flights from Bucharest: ~€120.” What she feels Warmth. Escape. Possibility. She doesn’t know which island. She doesn’t know a single route. But she’s intrigued. She clicks the Canary Islands. What she does NOT see No routes. No elevation profiles. No GPX files. The adventure has just begun. Alternative path: Raw heatmap mode Had Marta unticked the seasonality layer first, she would have seen pure year-round cycling activity across the globe. The Canaries would still glow—but so would Mallorca, Sardinia, and southern Portugal, all with strong annual heatmaps. She might have explored differently, drawn by raw popularity rather than seasonal fit. At any point, she could re-enable seasonality to ask: “OK, but is this place actually good in February?” The seasonal data becomes a post-factum validation of her curiosity rather than a pre-filter on her imagination. 7.3 Level 2: Canary Islands (Region) What Marta sees The globe zooms into the Canary archipelago. She sees the islands arranged geographically, each with a distinct identity card: Island Character Vibe Tenerife Volcanic giant. El Teide dominates. Dramatic altitude changes. The Canaries’ most famous cycling destination. Pro training. Serious climbers. Gran Canaria Diverse micro-climates. Beach to mountain in 30 minutes. Technical descents. All-rounder. Varied challenges. Fuerteventura Mars on Earth. Desert landscapes, endless coast, wind as a companion. The quiet one. Least crowded, most space. Adventurer. Solitude seeker. Gravel curious. Lanzarote Volcanic moonscape. Compact. Great roads. Club La Santa training base. Triathlete hub. Compact explorer. What she feels She’s drawn to Fuerteventura. “Mars on Earth” captures her imagination. “Least crowded, most space” speaks to her as a solo rider. She doesn’t want a pro-training camp—she wants an adventure. She clicks Fuerteventura. What she does NOT see Still no routes. She has chosen a place, not a ride. The platform has earned her emotional investment in Fuerteventura before showing her any logistics. 7.4 Level 3: Fuerteventura (Sub-Geography) What Marta sees The map zooms into the island. Fuerteventura reveals itself as a long, narrow landmass—100 km north to south—with distinct zones, each with its own color, character, and cycling personality: Zone: The North — Corralejo &amp; Dunes Surfer-town energy. Flat terrain with volcanic backdrops. The famous Corralejo dunes stretch along the coast. Easy-to-moderate riding on quiet roads. The gateway to Lanzarote (30-minute ferry). Best for: beginners, e-bike explorers, those who want beach time between rides. Zone: The Volcanic Interior — Betancuria &amp; Pájara The ancient heart of the island. Mars-like landscapes of red earth, dormant volcanoes, and wind-sculpted ravines. The climb to Betancuria—the island’s first colonial capital—is Fuerteventura’s iconic cycling challenge. The Morro Velosa viewpoint rewards suffering with panoramic views. Best for: experienced climbers, landscape photographers, history lovers. Zone: The Southeast — Las Playitas &amp; Gran Tarajal The serious cyclist’s base. Las Playitas sports resort is used by professional teams for winter training camps. Well-maintained roads. Structured training territory. Best for: performance-focused riders, triathletes, those who want a training community. Zone: The Wild South — Jandía Peninsula The entire peninsula is a nature reserve. Wild beaches at Cofete, reachable only by gravel track. The Pico de la Zarza rises to 807 m—the island’s highest point. Dramatic cliffs, untamed sea, and absolute solitude. Some segments require pushing the bike. Best for: gravel adventurers, those seeking wilderness, riders who want a story to tell. Zone: The West Coast — El Cotillo &amp; the Cliffs Remote and solitary. Coastal tracks overlooking furious waves. The ride from El Cotillo south to Jarugo is a series of ascents and descents above dramatic sea cliffs. Quieter than anywhere else on the island. Best for: solitude seekers, coastal lovers, those who find peace in wind. Contextual Signals (February-specific) Temperature: 17–23°C. Comfortable for all-day riding. Wind: Trade winds from the northeast, moderate. Cooling but manageable. Daylight: 11 hours. Sufficient for rides up to 100 km at touring pace. Calima risk: Low in February. Occasional Saharan dust—avoid riding on heavy dust days. Roads: Well-maintained asphalt. Inland roads quieter than coastal highways. Water: Scarce in the interior and south. Carry minimum 2 bottles. Refill in villages. Bike shops: Limited. Puerto del Rosario and Corralejo only. Bring essential spares. Rental: Available in Corralejo, Costa Calma, and Jandía. Book 1–2 weeks ahead in February. Base Suggestions The map highlights recommended bases with accommodation clusters: Base Character Best For Corralejo Surfer town. Restaurants, nightlife, bike rental. Ferry to Lanzarote. Easy access to north and interior. Mixed ability. Social base. Day trips to other zones by car. Costa Calma Quiet resort. Central location. Access to both Jandía and the interior. Solo riders wanting calm. Strategic position for covering the island. Las Playitas Sports resort. Training facilities. Pro-cyclist community. Performance riders. Structured training environment. What Marta feels She can now picture herself on this island. She knows the zones, the character, the vibe. The volcanic interior excites her. The wild south intrigues her. She doesn’t want a training camp—she picks Corralejo as her base, because it gives her access to the north for easy days and the interior for challenges, plus a car can reach the south for a Jandía adventure. What she does NOT see yet She has not seen a single specific route with an elevation profile. She has chosen her island, understood its zones, and picked a base. Only now is she ready for routes. 7.5 Level 4: Routes &amp; Hotspots (From Corralejo Base) What Marta finally sees Routes radiate from Corralejo on the map, categorized by effort and zone: Easy Morning Rides (&lt; 40 km) Corralejo Dunes Loop — 28 km. Flat coastal ride past the famous dunes. Paved. 1.5h. Wind exposure: moderate. Majanicho Beach Run — 22 km. Quiet north coast to a hidden beach. Paved + light gravel. 1h. Full Day Adventures (60–100 km) Corralejo → La Oliva → Betancuria — 85 km. The island’s iconic interior crossing. 900 m elevation gain. The Mars landscape. Colonial village. Morro Velosa viewpoint. 4–5h. North Coast: El Cotillo Circuit — 65 km. Coastal cliffs, surfer town, volcanic backroads. 600 m gain. 3–4h. Epic Challenges (100+ km) Norte a Sur: Corralejo → Morro Jable — 148 km. The full island traverse. Dunes, volcanoes, desert plains, Jandía peninsula. 1,500 m gain. 7–9h. Requires car shuttle return or overnight. Gravel Adventures (car shuttle from Corralejo) Cofete Wilderness — 48 km from Morro Jable. Gravel track into Jandía Nature Reserve. Wild beaches, 500 m ascent, some bike-pushing segments. 3–4h. Remote. Carry all water. Each route card shows: elevation profile, surface type per segment, water stops, phone signal, wind exposure, recent activity (“14 cyclists last week, rated 4.7”), and a seasonal note (“February: ideal. Low wind, no Calima.”). What Marta does She bookmarks 6 routes: both easy rides for recovery days, the Betancuria crossing as her signature challenge, the El Cotillo circuit for a coastal day, and the Cofete gravel adventure for the story. She doesn’t schedule them—she builds a menu. Weather and mood will decide each morning. What she feels Confidence. She knows the island’s character. She has a base, a flexible plan, and routes that match different energies. She books a flight and a hotel in Corralejo. She downloads the GPX pack. She’s going. 7.6 Case Study Summary: The Psychological Arc Level Marta Sees Marta Feels Time Spent L1 Globe Canaries glowing in February “I want warmth and escape.” 30 seconds L2 Region Four islands, each with personality “Fuerteventura speaks to me.” 2 minutes L3 Sub-geo Five zones, three bases, February conditions “I can picture myself in Corralejo.” 5–10 minutes L4 Routes Specific rides with elevation, logistics, ratings “I know what I’ll ride. I’m going.” 15–20 minutes Total time from “I don’t know where” to “I’m booking”: under 30 minutes. But the journey felt like an adventure in itself. The user didn’t browse a list of routes—she discovered a place, fell in love with it, and then chose how to explore it. That is the Velo-X experience. The platform mirrors the adventure it enables. 8. Promise Consistency Job 1 builds a promise. Job 2 delivers on it. If Marta was told “Mars on Earth” at L2 and “iconically challenging” at L3, the Betancuria route at L4 must deliver on that promise with accurate elevation data, honest difficulty, and Mars-like imagery. Consistency must hold across every level: globe, region card, sub-geography zone description, route card, and downloaded GPX. Broken promises destroy trust. For a solo cyclist in unfamiliar terrain, trust is the product. 9. Interaction Map Summary Open Velo-X. Globe auto-infers today’s date and shows the world’s cycling heartbeat right now. Quick Ideas show upcoming 1–3 month windows. Optionally override dates via calendar. Globe repaints for that window. Quick Ideas filter. User can clear override to snap back to today. Click a glowing region. Globe zooms to L2: Region view with island/area character cards. Select a sub-geography (island, city, valley). Continuous zoom to L3: Zones, bases, conditions. Select a base. L3 transitions to the Base View with accommodation, transport, zone access. Enter a zone. L4: Routes appear. Bookmark into flexible wish list by effort level. Commit: Book base (hotel + transport). Download GPX pack. Generate packing list. On the trip: open bookmarks daily, check weather, pick a route, ride. 10. What Velo-X Is Not Not a route database. Routes are the deepest layer, not the entry point. The user earns routes through progressive discovery. Not a navigation app. No turn-by-turn, no on-bike real-time features (yet). Not a social network. Heatmap data is aggregated, not personal. No feeds or followers. Not an itinerary builder. No day-by-day schedule. A flexible menu around a base. Not spammy. FOMO is built on real data—weather windows, accommodation fill rates, seasonal access. Velo-X is a psychological adventure that leads to a physical one. The platform mirrors the journey it enables: wonder, attraction, familiarity, confidence, commitment.
